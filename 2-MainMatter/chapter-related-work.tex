\subsection{SONs}
Semantic Overlay Networks are sophisticated structured overlays based on semantics – each node maintains connections with other nodes influenced by their content, nodes having many "Jazz" files will connect to other similar nodes. SONs are, optionally, hierarchically structured, so a \textit{father SON} with category "Music" mantains a unidirectional connection to \textit{childreen SONs} containing any genre of music, and so on. By having this structure, queries, that enter the system, are classified and only routed to the SON in respect to it's semantics, thus preventing the wastefullness of flooding (maybe mention article – idk). It's also important to note, that nodes are not fixed to just one SON, a node can be part of multiple SONs based on the content it holds. To join a SON, a node must contain a significant percentage of the content related to the SON's category – Layered SONs — with fallback for higher levels of the hierachy when there are insufficient results.

In the original paper, the authors acknowledged the limitation of their algorithm to be regarding \textit{Completeness} – there are no guarantees that the search of a document will 100\% retrive it, due to missclassification of documents (which has a probability of 25\% of happening, and 4\% of missclassification of nodes), forcing the system to adopt a flooding technique between all the nodes in the overlay, to be 100\% sure that the document is retrieved. Furthermore, it contains another crucial limitation, \textit{Granularity}. The authors state that the level of granularity has to be carefully decided, as too little granularity will not generate enough locality (one node belongs to all SONs), while too much would increase maintenance costs (and one SON would contain every node).

\subsection{SP}
SemanticPeer tackles the problem at its root – the exact lookup. Instead of enhancing the structure of the nodes, it suggests a novel distributed lookup protocol, they propose an approximate matching model where participants agree on a distributed model of word meaning that maps terms to a vector space. The terms are mapped to word embeddings and the space is particioned per agglomerative hierarchical clustering with cosine distance – resulting in a \textit{Dendogram}.

Each node is responsible for his own branch of the dendogram and its identifier is the mean vector of all the vectors it stores. Upon entering the system, a node receives half the branch of another, and both exchange routing references – routing table with entries of pair <<mean vector, IP address>>. It is important to note, that the father of the branch is not aware of the split that just happened, thus his routing table has maximum size of O(log N).

The presented lookup is iterative and greedy. To effectively search the searching vector is compared with the identifiers in the routing table of the current branch. Then sends the lookup message to the nearest K nodes, using the cosine distance (which lacks the unidrirectionality of conventional metrics (i.e Euclidean distance over Chord's circular identifiers space or XOR metric over Kademlia's binary tree)) – each responding with the k nearest that it knows and returning back to the initiator to repeat the process. The stopping condition is when the k nodes identified in a iteration are equal tho those identified in the previous one, in other words, when they converge. Thus, "cat" and "kitty" end up in the same node or in neighooring nodes, such a thing is impossible with hashing.

To design such a neat algorithm it is impossible to cover everything. The authors, explicitly excluded churn and faults, limiting their algorithm to "just" static networks; their routing algorithm is revolutionary, although not flawless, it is based on probabilistics and not on deterministic functions – heuristic clustering, mean vector as imperfect representation; and all of this is seen with the recall of messages, varying between 66\% and 45\% with K=1, maxing out at 97\% with K=20 in a network of 1000 nodes. Futhermore, there is a structural limit worth mentioning, the maximum size of the network is determined a priori by the size of vector space and by the number of clusters which the dendogram can generate.
