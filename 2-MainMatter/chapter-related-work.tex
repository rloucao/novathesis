\chapter{Related Work}
\label{chap:related-work}
Searching across federated scientific repositories touches on several bodies of work that have developed largely in isolation. Structured overlays gave us efficient and fully decentralised lookup, but only for keys that are known exactly. The information retrieval community solved resource selection and result merging for collections that will not surrender their data, but assumed a coordinator and cooperative participants. Work on semantic peer-to-peer search attacked the exact-match limitation from two different angles, while the security community catalogued what happens to any of these systems once some participants stop playing by the rules. Each line answers part of the question this dissertation asks; none answers it entirely.

\subsection{SONs}
\label{sec:SONs}
Semantic Overlay Networks are sophisticated structured overlays based on semantics – each node maintains connections with other nodes influenced by their content, nodes having many "Jazz" files will connect to other similar nodes. SONs are, optionally, hierarchically structured, so a \textit{father SON} with category "Music" mantains a unidirectional connection to \textit{children SONs} containing any genre of music, and so on. By having this structure, queries, that enter the system, are classified and only routed to the SON in respect to it's semantics, thus preventing the wastefullness of flooding \cite{crespo2005sons}. It's also important to note, that nodes are not fixed to just one SON, a node can be part of multiple SONs based on the content it holds. To join a SON, a node must contain a significant percentage of the content related to the SON's category – \textit{Layered SONs} — with fallback for higher levels of the hierachy when there are insufficient results.

In the original paper, the authors acknowledged the limitation of their algorithm to be regarding \textit{Completeness} – there are no guarantees that the search of a document will 100\% retrive it, due to missclassification of documents (which has a probability of 25\% of happening, and 4\% of missclassification of nodes), forcing the system to adopt a flooding technique between all the nodes in the overlay, to be 100\% sure that the document is retrieved. Furthermore, it contains another crucial limitation, \textit{Granularity}. The authors state that the level of granularity has to be carefully decided, as too little granularity will not generate enough locality (one node belongs to all SONs), while too much would increase maintenance costs (and one SON would contain every node).

SONs show that organising nodes by content pays off, but the approach rests on a classification that everyone must agree on and that is defined upfront. Nothing in the design accounts for taxonomies that evolve, for content that legitimately belongs to several categories at once, or for nodes that might lie about the category they claim to hold.

\subsection{SP}
\label{sec:SP}
SemanticPeer tackles the problem at its root – the exact lookup. Instead of enhancing the structure of the nodes, it suggests a novel distributed lookup protocol, they propose an approximate matching model where participants agree on a distributed model of word meaning that maps terms to a vector space. The terms are mapped to word embeddings and the space is particioned per agglomerative hierarchical clustering with cosine distance – resulting in a \textit{Dendogram}.

Each node is responsible for his own branch of the dendrogram and its identifier is the mean vector of all the vectors it stores. Upon entering the system, a node receives half the branch of another, and both exchange routing references – routing table with entries of pair <<mean vector, IP address>>. It is important to note, that the father of the branch is not aware of the split that just happened, thus his routing table has maximum size of O(log N).

The presented lookup is iterative and greedy. To effectively search, the searching vector is compared with the identifiers in the routing table of the current branch. Then the father node, sends the lookup message to the nearest K nodes, using the cosine distance (which lacks the unidrirectionality of conventional metrics (i.e Euclidean distance over Chord's circular identifiers space or XOR metric over Kademlia's binary tree)) – each responding with the k nearest that it knows and returning back to the initiator to repeat the process. The stopping condition is when the k nodes identified in a iteration are equal to those identified in the previous one, in other words, when they converge. Thus, "cat" and "kitty" end up in the same or in neighooring nodes, such a thing is impossible with Consistent Hashing.

To design such a neat algorithm it is impossible to cover everything. The authors, explicitly excluded churn and faults, limiting their algorithm to "just" static networks; their routing algorithm is revolutionary, although not flawless, it is based on probabilistics and not on deterministic functions – heuristic clustering, mean vector as imperfect representation; and all of this is seen with the recall of messages, varying between 66\% and 45\% with K=1, maxing out at 97\% with K=20 in a network of 1000 nodes. Futhermore, there is a structural limit worth mentioning, the maximum size of the network is determined a priori by the size of vector space and by the number of clusters the dendogram can generate.

SemanticPeer removes the need for a shared taxonomy, but trades it for a different set of assumptions: a network with no churn and no failures, and participants that honestly advertise where they sit in the semantic space. Neither holds once the system leaves the lab.

\subsubsection{OpenPubSub}
\label{sec:OpenPubSub}
OpenPubSub is an extentasion of SP into the world of P2P Publish-Subscribe systems, tackling the core issues of it. With SP mapping terms into nodes, the approximate lookup can generate \textit{Stray Topics} – nodes far from where semantically related content lives, so they never meet the subscriptions that should match them. OpenPubSub solves this issue by adopting a hybrid routing algorithm: rendezvous routing (similar to how SP routes); and introducing gossip routing between nodes that have similar subscriptions.

Before we proceed, we must answer the question: What is gossip routing? In a very simple way, it just means that a node (that doesn't contain a view of the entire system) broadcast a message to (some or) all of the nodes it knows, and all of the others do the same, until the expected condition is met. 

OpenPubSub uses gossip with two layers: random peer sampling, based on Cyclon \cite{voulgaris2005cyclon}, and peer selection on top of it, based on Vicinity \cite{voulgaris2013vicinity}: Random peer sampling (random exchange of partials views); Peer selection (choosing the neighbours based on similar subscriptions); An event arriving to the rendezvous is then sent to the neighbours of that node, that most likely have similar subscriptions.

With the hybrid routing, the upper bound for approximate event matching recall is 56.7\%; rendezvous routing alone reaches up to 54\% while cutting messaging overhead by 44\%, whereas the hybrid approach reaches 43.8\% with a 59\% reduction. The hybrid trades recall for overhead rather than improving both.

Although OpenPubSub improves on SemanticPeer, it still leaves three aspects unattended, and all of them matter for federated scientific repositories. First, everyone is assumed to be cooperative – there is no threat model, and nothing stops a node from advertising a position in the semantic space that has nothing to do with what it actually holds. Second, it is publish/subscribe, meaning standing subscriptions waiting for events to flow by. Searching a federation is the other way around, ad-hoc queries against data that is already sitting there, which needs persistent indexes, ranking and result merging instead of event dissemination. Third, and this applies to everything we have seen so far, every peer is treated as interchangeable – there is no notion of who owns what, who is allowed to publish it, or who answers for it.
